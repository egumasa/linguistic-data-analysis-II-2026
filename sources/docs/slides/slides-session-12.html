Step 1 — Sample

Pool vs. sample

A pool is everything the corpus has, with its natural imbalance:

cefr_pool.json → A1: 51 · A2: 246 · B1: 1229 · B2: 1188 · C1: 436 · C2: 33

A sample is what you study — equal items per label:

sampled = sample_pool(pool, N_PER_CLASS, SEED)
# Sampled 42 items. Per-label counts: {'A1': 7, 'A2': 7, ... 'C2': 7}

Why balance

With 1229 B1s and 33 C2s, a model that answers “B1” to every item gets a high accuracy score without using any information.

Balanced sampling makes per-class precision and recall readable, and makes the confusion matrix show which labels are confused with which instead of showing the class sizes.

Note

Rare classes give you fewer items. That is a property of the data, and it belongs in your limitations section.

Why keep the pool separate

The items you did not sample have a purpose: they become your few-shot examples in 04_develop.

If your examples came from your gold set, the model would be given the answers to the items you are about to test it on.

build_fewshot prevents this for you, but only if items remain in the pool. Sample from the full pool, not the demo file.

Step 2 — QC

The step you must do yourself

The published labels are somebody else’s judgment.

Before you measure a model against them, two of you re-annotate the sample independently and blind. You then measure how far apart you were, and discuss every item you labelled differently.

These are the same A–F steps you did by hand in Session 5, now on your own track.

What “blind” means here

create_annotation_sheet deliberately does not copy the published label across.

| ID | Text                          | CoderA | CoderB | Final | Note |
|----|-------------------------------|--------|--------|-------|------|
| 1  | Bears do not have good hearing|        |        |       |      |

CoderA and CoderB fill their own columns without looking at each other’s. Final stays empty until you have talked.

Tip

The Sheet is a real Google Sheet, so all group members can work in it at the same time.

Then measure — and you write these lines

rows = load_coder_sheets(SHEET_ID, CODERS)   # one tab per coder, joined by ID

a = column(rows, CODERS[0])
b = column(rows, CODERS[1])

percent_agreement(a, b)                      # 71.4%
print(cohen_kappa_score(a, b))               # 0.657

Which κ you owe is not a free choice, and it is settled before you run anything — by how many coders you have and by whether PLAN.md §3 says your labels are a scale. Two coders, unordered: cohen_kappa_score(a, b). On a scale: weights="quadratic" as well. Three or more: fleiss_kappa.

Both numbers, not one. Percent agreement alone counts lucky agreement as if you had earned it; κ alone is hard to read without the raw figure beside it. They go in report section 1.

What counts as a disagreement? — you write that too

disagreements is not given to you, because the rule inside it is a decision about your scheme:

disagreed = disagreements(rows, coders=CODERS)   # your function

A row is a disagreement when your coders did not all choose the same label — that is the obvious rule. On labels that sit on a scale, you may decide only a gap of two or more is worth arguing about. Both are defensible; which you used is a sentence in your report.

Tip

You saw these six lines on Day 2 S5, step E. answer("disagreements") is there if you stall.

Adjudicate

The 12 rows you disagreed on are not random error. They are the places where your scheme is unclear, and they are usually the same places where the model makes errors.

For each: agree a Final label, and note why. Then:

rows = load_coder_sheets(SHEET_ID, CODERS)          # re-read: Final is filled in now
gold = to_canonical(rows, LABELS, source=sampled)   # puts back what the sheet drops
compare_to_published(gold, sampled)
# 37 / 42 match the published label (88.1%)

Those 5 differences are a finding about the corpus, not an error on your part.

The consequence

Your adjudicated gold is what the model gets scored against

Not the published labels.

This is why this session matters. The label boundaries you discuss here are the ones you will refer to on Day 5, in 06_report, when you explain the model’s errors.

Step 3 — Draw the line

Which items you may look at

Tomorrow you will change your prompt because of what you saw it get wrong. That is the work. But a score measured on the items you kept adjusting against stops measuring how good your prompt is, and starts measuring how long you kept adjusting.

So the line gets drawn now, before anything has been run against these items:

what it is for
dev the items you may look at. Iterate here, as many rounds as you like
test opened once, in 05_test.ipynb. Whatever it says is what you report

The split

dev, test = split_dev_test(gold, DEV, seed=SEED)

save_json(dev,  DEV_PATH,  what="dev items")
save_json(test, TEST_PATH, what="test items")

Read the per-label counts it prints before you save. A label that lands in dev but not in test cannot appear in the score you report, and this is the last cheap moment to change dev: in config.yaml and draw the line again.

Note

This is the loop you wrote by hand this morning, in Day 4 Part B — bucket by label, take some of each. One call now.

dev is the fast set

You do not shrink your sample to iterate

One round on the dev half — a dozen or so items — is about a minute of enforced pacing. Your sample stays at full size all the way through, and the number you finally report comes off the test half, which nothing has touched.

There is no right ratio at this size, only one you can defend. A bigger dev gives steadier feedback and leaves a smaller test, so the reported number bounces more. A smaller dev means prompt decisions made on very few items — which is how you tune to noise.
