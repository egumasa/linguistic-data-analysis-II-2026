Error analysis

The number shows the change; the errors show the reason

macro-F1 went from 0.31 to 0.34. What changed?

You cannot answer that from the average. You have to look at what the model actually got wrong.

errors = show_errors(val, pred_cot)
errors.head(15)

Four columns: id · gold · pred · text.

Read down the F1 column first

evaluate already printed per-class F1. One or two levels will sit far below the rest.

That is where macro-F1 is being lost. Because macro-F1 weights every class equally, a rare class with F1 near zero lowers the average as much as a common one does.

Then read three actual sentences from that level. The counts show you where the problem is; the sentences show you what it is.

Two different causes

“The model is wrong”

The sentence is clearly B1, the model said C1, and your scheme is unambiguous about it. → a prompt problem

“The scheme is fuzzy”

You and your partner would also disagree about this sentence. → not fixable by prompting

Distinguishing these two cases is the most important analytic skill in your final report.

Iterating the prompt

The same change you already saw

Back in S7, Huang & Mizumoto’s Ex 1 → Ex 2 was a generic prompt tightened into a structured one.

That is the same procedure as here, except that now the change is based on your error data, not on intuition:

  1. read the errors (show_errors) — where does the prompt fail?
  2. tighten one thing — usually the Criteria / label descriptors.
  3. re-measure macro-F1 — did it actually help?

Tip

Change one thing per round, so you know what moved the number.

Say what you expect, before you run it

Three lines, filled in before you touch the prompt:

WORST_CLASS = "…"   # the level with the lowest F1
MY_CHANGE   = "…"   # the ONE thing you will change
I_PREDICT   = "…"   # what you expect, and why

This is what makes the work a study. A change that helps for a reason you stated in advance is a finding; a change that helps for no reason you can state is not.

An incorrect prediction does not lower your mark. Not stating a prediction at all does.

Check the class you aimed at

Macro-F1 can rise while the level you targeted gets worse, because a different class improved and raised the average.

So compare the F1 row for WORST_CLASS before and after, not only the macro-F1.

# Change Why I expected it to help macro-F1 Target F1 Right?
0 (no change) — … … —
1 … … … … …

Two recorded rounds with stated reasons are worth more than five undocumented ones.

Inside run_prompt

What run_prompt has been doing

We read the helper you have called all day. You do not edit it; the purpose is that you can describe what it does.

  1. Insert each sentence at the {text} position → one prompt per item.
  2. Call the model, paced so you stay under 15 requests/minute.
  3. On a 429, wait and retry; on a daily quota error, stop immediately.
  4. Scan the reply for the first CEFR level and keep it.
  5. Return a plain list of labels, in gold order.

Step 4 is the step that most easily fails, which is why the CoT prompt forbids mentioning other levels.

The LLM is one option, not the only one

How good is 0.36, really?

Arase, Uchida & Kajiwara (2022) — the paper behind this corpus — trained models on this exact task (their Table 6):

System macro-F1 weighted κ
Bag-of-words + SVM 41.2 0.354
k-NN over frozen BERT 38.8 0.373
BERT-Base, loss-weighted 82.5 0.609
Their proposed model 84.5 0.628
Our zero-shot prompt (72 items) ≈36 0.849

Two readings you should not avoid

1. Zero-shot prompting scores near bag-of-words, not near BERT.

The difference up to 84.5 is what a labelled training set gives you. Building CEFR-SP required trained experts, and that is what such a training set costs.

2. Our weighted κ looks better than theirs. It is not.

Their test set is 3,012 sentences with the natural imbalance; ours is a balanced 72. κ is sensitive to the marginals.

A higher number obtained on a different test set is not a valid comparison, and your report must not make it.

What this licenses

Important

“The model got 0.36” means nothing until you can say 0.36 compared to what, on which items.

Claimable: “a zero-shot prompt reaches roughly bag-of-words performance on this task”.

Not claimable: “our prompt scored higher than the published model on agreement”.

Assistant, not replacement

Neither approach is “the answer” — each has costs: data to train vs. a prompt to write, reproducibility, interpretability.

And back to where the running example started — Huang & Mizumoto (2025):

“Human teachers must ultimately remain at the core of language teaching and learning.”

The LLM is a tool you evaluate and supervise. You do not accept its output without checking it.
