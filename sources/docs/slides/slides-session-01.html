1 · Why this course

LLMs are everywhere now

Large language models are now common tools — for searching, translating, coding, and more.

So the question isn’t whether linguists will use them. It’s how to use them responsibly for linguistic analysis.

Linguistic annotation: promising but costly

One especially promising use is annotation — labelling language data for analysis.

  • But careful annotation is expensive: expert time, training, guidelines, double-coding.
  • If an LLM could annotate reliably, then it facilitates research.

So the question arises: can an LLM do this annotation for us?

One simple Goal of the course

Detour — Brief history of embedding

Before LLM — Brief history of embedding

LLM encodes language into numbers — The fundational NLP technique called embedding.

Embedding: “vector representations of the meaning of words that are learned directly from word distributions in texts” (Jurafsky & Martin, 2026, p. 96).

Two families, and we build up to both:

  • Static — one fixed vector per word: counting contexts, then Word2Vec.
  • Contextual — a different vector each time the word appears: BERT, and the models you use.

RGB color space

A vector is a list of numbers that fixes the location of something in a space. A colour, for example, is fixed by how much red, green and blue it has.

Move the sliders to change the colour. Drag the cube to rotate it.

The dashed path is the vector: R along red, then G along green, then B along blue. The number under each name is the distance from your colour to that colour — close in the space = similar colour. Word embeddings work the same way, with hundreds of numbers instead of three.

“You shall know a word by the company it keeps!” (Firth, 1957, p. 11)

Finding the latent dimensions

We can apply factor analysis to a co-occurrence table to reduce meaning.

Factor analysis

A statistical method for uncovering the latent structure of data:

  • We give a 6-item questionnaire on enjoyment and boredom.
  • The questionnaire items are the observed variables.
  • We find latent variables that predict the values of the observed variables.
Each arrow is a loading. Solid = strong, dashed = near zero. Values are illustrative.

Six items, two latent variables. Each item loads high on one and near zero on the other.

Finding latent dimension of word meaning

The words in corpus are the observed variables, and we explore underlying dimensions of that.

The same diagram as the questionnaire. Context words are the observed variables; the dimensions are what factor analysis extracts.

. . .

Each dimension gives every context word a loading — how much that word counts towards it:

dimension 1 dimension 2
computer 0.651 −0.009
data 0.756 0.004
result 0.066 0.019
pie 0.001 0.998
sugar 0.002 0.061

result loads on neither. Not every word belongs to a dimension.

Nobody assigned these weights — they were derived from the corpus counts. This technique is called Latent Semantic Analysis (LSA).

word2vec: We actually do not need table

LSA provided the support for distributional semantics.

But it is usually applied to document-level co-occurrence, and very coarse meaning dimensions. The table is huge and difficult to use large corpus.

Also, co-occurrence pattern is local phenomena.

word2vec learns a word’s vector by predicting it from its neighbours

The contexts go in at the top, the guess comes out at the bottom, and the layer in between holds the 300 numbers we keep.

The model is given the words around a gap and has to guess the word that was removed.

It works through billions of gaps like this one. Each time the guess is wrong, the 300 numbers in the middle are adjusted a little.

After training, the guess is not what we want — the output layer is discarded. What we keep is the middle layer: the 300 numbers a word arrives with. Words that fill the same kinds of gaps end up with similar numbers.

Still one vector per word form. free in “your account is free of charge” and FREE in “claim your FREE prize now” get the same 300 numbers.

BERT: word2vec with a transformer

Same training idea — fill in the blank — but the vector for a word is now computed from the sentence around it, using the attention step the video described. One word form no longer gets one fixed vector.

Here is what that does to the pair from the last slide:

Sentence free attends most to Resulting sense
“your account is free of charge” account, charge banking, benign
“claim your FREE prize now” claim, prize, now promotional

The same word form now has two different vectors, because the surrounding words differ. Static embeddings could not do this; there was only ever one vector for free.

Why this matters for you

This is the whole reason an LLM can annotate at all.

The categories you care about — is this stance hedged?, is this move a Gap or a Purpose?, what CEFR level is this? — are not properties of the words in isolation. They are properties of words in context.

A bag-of-words model cannot represent that. A transformer can.

Warning

Being able to represent context is not the same as producing correct labels. Measuring that difference is what the rest of the week is about.

The paradigm shift

Classic NLP consumed annotation to build models.

Now the model can produce annotation, so the direction is reversed:

before: humans annotate → train the model now: the model annotates → and who checks it?

What people have already done with this

Historically each task had its own model — a POS tagger, an NER model, a parser. Now one model does many tasks, because you change the request rather than the model (Liu et al., 2023).

That is not a promise. It is a research literature, and you can read it:

Study What it annotated Track you could pick
Yu et al. (2024), IJCL apology components — a function, not a form pragmatics
Kim & Lu (2024), JEAP rhetorical moves in article abstracts RAAMove · CaRS-50

Three of these are on your readings page. Four of them do a task you could choose for your own project.

What an LLM lets you do

Three things, and they are the reason the rest of the week is worth the effort.

  1. It can label categories that depend on context. Most of the categories linguists work with are of that kind, and a bag-of-words model could never represent them.

  2. One model, many tasks. A new question needs a new prompt, not a new trained model and a new annotated training set.

  3. Careful annotation — expert time, training, double-coding — used to need more resources than a small project has. A five-day project can now attempt it.

None of that says the labels are correct. That is the next question, and it is the rest of the course.

6 · But — we need evaluation

…and where it did not work

The same literature reports the other half.

  • Curry, Baker & Brookes (2024), Applied Corpus Linguistics — passable at grouping keywords, but poor at concordance analysis: it made false inferences, and it altered the data it was given.

  • Ziems et al. (2024), Computational Linguistics — 13 models across 25 benchmarks. It scores lower than trained supervised models on classification, and higher than crowd workers on open-ended coding. Fair, not expert.

  • Crosthwaite & Baisa (2023) — with a corpus you can say which texts your evidence came from. With an LLM you cannot.

So: same tool, opposite results, depending on the task. The only way to know which case you are in is to measure it.

Sounding right is not being right

An LLM essentially learns to predict next plausible word.

Plausible is not the same as correct — which is exactly what Curry et al. found when the output read well and the analysis was wrong.

That’s why we still need NLP evaluation.

What this course covers

Important

This course is the evaluation half of NLP, applied to LLMs.

We do not cover the training part of NLP. The evaluation part provides exactly the rigour an LLM’s outputs need, and that is what you will learn to run this week.

  • inter-annotator agreement (percent + Cohen’s κ)
  • precision / recall / F1, confusion matrix
