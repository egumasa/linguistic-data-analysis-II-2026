📋 Agenda

We open with ten minutes in the Arase paper — pulling out the two design decisions S4 left for you. Then you build a gold standard for your track: the graded construction skill, split across three surfaces, with one spine (A–F) running through all of them:

  • These slides teach the concepts — how to sample, why annotate blind, how to read a matrix.
  • A Google Sheet holds the human judgment — you and your partner annotate there.

Note

This is the construction skill — graded once, here. Give the annotation real care.

🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this session, you will be able to:

  • Working in a pair, each annotate the same ~20 sentences by hand against a prepared scheme.
  • Import the sheet into Colab and compute percent agreement, Cohen’s κ, and an annotator-vs-annotator confusion matrix.
  • Read the confusion matrix to see where you disagree, then refine the scheme and re-annotate — iterate until agreement is acceptable.
  • Adjudicate the remaining disagreements, compare to the published gold, and interpret the differences.
  • Export your annotations as a canonical {id, text, label} gold set.

What you found

Who is allowed to annotate

Their claim: sentence CEFR level can be judged objectively — but only by someone with teaching experience. So they tested that before trusting anyone.

  • Pilot — 228 sentences. Against a senior expert (12 yrs teaching): a CEFR-trained grad student reached r = 0.74. Three grad students with no CEFR and no teaching background: 0.45 / 0.50 / 0.59.
  • Trial — 8 candidates, the same 100 sentences, scored against the expert’s labels. Agreement ran 0.59–0.77 — and roughly tracked years and type of teaching experience.
  • Kept 2, on two criteria: high agreement (r = .75, .73) and small average level difference (0.11, 0.22).

Note

Why both? Correlation asks do you rank sentences the same way. Mean difference asks are you calibrated — a rater who is consistently one grade high correlates almost perfectly.

Which sentences are allowed in

The rule: if you need anything outside the sentence to judge it, the sentence doesn’t belong.

“The white house announced his return.” — easy words, easy grammar. But you must know the White House is an organisation, and resolve his from the sentence before. That’s world knowledge and discourse, not sentence difficulty.

  • First sentence of a paragraph only → nothing to co-refer back to.
  • No named entities, quotations or brackets — though dates, numbers and famous places are allowed.
  • 5–30 words, then sampled at random.
  • A third source on purpose: Newsela-Auto + Wiki-Auto (8.5k each) — plus SCoRE (3.0k), written for beginners, because A-level sentences are otherwise almost absent.

Tip

These filters weren’t decided up front — they ran several rounds of manual checks over a few hundred samples before freezing them. Sampling is something you debug.

And what came out of it

Sentences the annotators agreed on in the pilot and trial became the example per level in the guidelines — so selecting people also produced the scheme’s anchors (Arase et al., Table 1):

CEFR Level Example sentence
A1 She had a beautiful necklace around her neck.
A2 Some experts say the classes should be changed.
B1 Historically there have also been negative consequences.
B2 Alligators are generally timid towards humans and tend to walk or swim away if one approaches.

Tip

Not just word difficulty — grammar, abstraction and world knowledge climb too. This is the judgment you’re about to make. Their own two experts matched exactly 37.6% of the time — so expect to differ, from your partner and from the gold.

Now you do it — one spine, three surfaces

Today’s goal, as six steps (A–F)

Your goal: build a gold standard for your track, and know how far you can trust it. That is phase ③ (Eguchi & Kyle Step 6) — broken into six steps. The same A–F labels appear on every surface, so you always know where you are — find your place by the letter, not the slide number.

Step Where you work What happens
A · Sample slide + Sheet concept: how to draw a representative ~20; you copy your track’s sheet
B · Apply the scheme slide + guidelines restate the decidable rule you’ll annotate against
C · Annotate blind Sheet each partner labels the same items, independently
D · Measure agreement Colab + slide % agreement · Cohen’s κ · annotator-vs-annotator matrix
E · Read → refine → re-annotate slide + Sheet where do you diverge? fix the scheme, re-label
F · Adjudicate → gold Colab + slide resolve, compare to published, export {id,text,label}

Tip

You bounce out to the Sheet twice (annotate in C, re-annotate in E) and back. Colab opens at D and stays open. The slides run alongside as your interpretation reference.

A · Sample — represent the domain (E&K Step 3 · ①)

Draw a sample that stands for the domain

A gold standard is only as trustworthy as the sample it’s built on. Before anyone labels anything, decide what you’re drawing.

  • Representative — cover the range you care about (proficiency, L1, register) so your κ isn’t an accident of an easy slice.
  • Right-sized — enough to measure agreement, small enough to double-annotate by hand (~20 today).
  • Reproducible — a fixed random seed means the “same 20” every time; anyone can re-draw your exact sample.
  • One fixed unit — one label per unit of analysis (here, the sentence). Settle this before you start.
  • Exclusions that defend the unit — Arase’s first-sentence-only, no named entities rule, which you just found. Anything judged from outside the sentence doesn’t belong in the pool.

Note

Today the sample is already drawn for you — one sheet per track. You’ll learn how it was sampled here, and just copy it in a moment. (The draw itself lives in Colab as a reference cell.)

B · Apply the operationalized scheme (E&K Steps 4–5; Fuoli · ②)

Restate the rule before you label

You already have an operationalized scheme from the earlier sessions — a decidable rule a second person can follow. Now you apply it.

  • Restate the rule and the unit — one label per unit; know your label set cold.
  • Skim the guidelines and per-level examples before you annotate, not while you annotate.
  • Don’t invent as you go — if the rule can’t decide a case, that’s a note for step E, not a private house rule.

This is ① and ② made concrete: a decidable rule, applied to a real sample.

Copy your track’s sheet, then go annotate

Open your track’s template Sheet → File → Make a copy into your own Drive. Note the copied title — Colab needs it in step D. Then move to step C.

C · Annotate blind, in pairs (E&K Step 6 · ③)

Two people, same items, no peeking

This is the heart of it, and it happens entirely in the Sheet — no code.

  • Each partner labels the same ~20 items in the sheet you copied.
  • Two annotator columns (label_a, label_b) → agreement falls straight out in step D.
  • Blind = no peeking at your partner’s column, no discussing as you go.

The disagreements are the point — they show where the scheme is still fuzzy. Don’t smooth them over now.

Note

Your turn: label all ~20 rows in your own column, independently. Use the notes column for anything you found genuinely hard to decide — those notes are your evidence in step E.

D · Measure agreement (E&K Step 6 · ③)

Percent agreement vs. Cohen’s κ

Now Colab opens for the first time. It reads your sheet and reports three numbers — but the reading of them happens here.

  • Percent agreement — the raw diagonal. Easy, but flattering: two coders who both say “B1” most of the time agree a lot by luck.
  • Cohen’s κ — agreement corrected for chance. This is the honest number.

Tip

Recall from S4: 80% raw agreement was only κ ≈ 0.52 once chance was removed. Trust the κ.

→ run this in step D (Colab): load_annotation_sheet → annotator_agreement.

Read the confusion matrix

The κ tells you how much you disagree. The annotator-vs-annotator confusion matrix tells you where.

  • Rows = annotator A, columns = annotator B — one cell per label pair.
  • The diagonal is where you agreed; off-diagonal cells are your disagreements.
  • A cluster in one off-diagonal cell (say B1 ↔︎ B2) is a signpost: that boundary is under-defined.

Don’t just note that κ is low — find the specific label pair dragging it down. That cell is your to-do list for step E.

E · Read the matrix → refine → re-annotate (E&K Step 6; Fuoli princ. 2 · ③)

Fix the scheme, then label again

A confusion matrix isn’t a verdict on your annotating — it’s a diagnosis of your scheme.

  • Take the off-diagonal cells — which label pairs get confused?
  • Refine the scheme / guidelines so that ambiguity becomes decidable (add a rule, a boundary case, an example).
  • Re-annotate the affected items; recompute κ. Iterate until agreement is acceptable.

This is Fuoli’s principle 2 in action — guidelines are tested and refined until reliable. If two trained coders still can’t agree, no model can be trusted on it either.

Re-annotate in a fresh round tab

Make your scheme edits, then duplicate the round1 tab → round2 and re-label the confused items there — never overwrite round1. Back in Colab, disagreements(rows) lists what to revisit; set ROUND = "round2" and re-run step D to see κ move. Each round is preserved, so the analysis stays reproducible and you watch κ climb round over round.

F · Adjudicate → gold (E&K Step 6 → feeds ④⑤)

Resolve, compare, export

The last disagreements don’t refine away — you decide them. That’s adjudication, and it’s the graded step.

  • Talk through the remaining disagreements and agree a single final label for each.
  • Compare to the published gold — where do you differ, and why? A difference isn’t automatically an error.
  • Export the canonical {id, text, label} set — this is S6’s yardstick, and the input to ④⑤.

Note

Differing from the published gold ≠ being wrong. Arase’s own experts agreed exactly only 37.6% of the time. What matters is that your disagreement is defensible — that’s the argument your mini-project has to make.

→ run this in step F (Colab): to_canonical → compare_to_published → save to Drive.
